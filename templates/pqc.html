What is Post-Quantum Cryptography?

Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQC) refers to cryptographic algorithms that are designed to be secure against attacks from both classical and quantum computers. Today's encryption standards — RSA, ECC (Elliptic Curve Cryptography), and Diffie-Hellman — rely on mathematical problems that are very hard for classical computers to solve. But a sufficiently powerful quantum computer running Shor's Algorithm can solve these problems in polynomial time, effectively breaking them.

PQC replaces these vulnerable algorithms with new ones based on mathematical problems believed to be resistant even to quantum computers — like lattice problems, hash functions, and error-correcting codes.

A Simple Analogy

Imagine your front door lock (RSA encryption) is unbreakable by any human thief (classical computer). Now imagine someone invents a master key machine (quantum computer) that can crack any lock in seconds. PQC is like installing a brand-new type of lock that the master key machine simply cannot work on.

The "Harvest Now, Decrypt Later" (HNDL) Threat

Nation-state adversaries are already collecting encrypted internet traffic today with the intent to decrypt it once quantum computers become powerful enough. This means data encrypted right now — medical records, financial transactions, classified communications — is at risk in the future. The clock has already started.

Why Do We Need PQC?

The need for PQC is driven by three converging forces: the advancing power of quantum hardware, the timelines for cryptographic migration, and the long-lived sensitivity of data.

  • Quantum Hardware Progress: IBM, Google, and China's national labs have demonstrated 1,000+ qubit processors. Cryptographically relevant quantum computers are estimated to arrive within 10–15 years.
  • Cryptographic Migration Takes Time: Migrating the entire internet's infrastructure took 10+ years for TLS 1.2 → 1.3. PQC migration must start now.
  • Long-Lived Sensitive Data: Government secrets, medical records, and intellectual property need to remain confidential for 25–50 years — long after quantum computers arrive.
  • NIST's Mandate: The US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) finalised the first PQC standards in August 2024, signalling it is no longer theoretical.
  • India's Digital Future: India's UPI, Aadhaar, DigiLocker and banking systems process billions of encrypted transactions daily — all vulnerable to quantum attacks without migration.

Did You Know?

The NSA announced in 2022 that it would not approve new RSA or ECC deployments for national security systems, explicitly citing the quantum threat. The private sector is expected to follow.

NIST PQC Standards (2024)

After an 8-year global competition, NIST standardised four PQC algorithms in August 2024. These are the algorithms the world will migrate to:

ML-KEM

Key Encapsulation

Based on CRYSTALS-Kyber. Used to securely exchange encryption keys over a public channel. The PQC replacement for RSA and Diffie-Hellman key exchange. Fast and compact — ideal for web and mobile.

ML-DSA

Digital Signature

Based on CRYSTALS-Dilithium. Used to verify that a message or piece of software genuinely came from who it claims. Replaces RSA and ECDSA signatures used in certificates and code signing.

SLH-DSA

Digital Signature

Based on SPHINCS+. A hash-based signature scheme with the simplest security assumptions — it relies only on the security of the hash function, not complex algebraic structures.

FN-DSA

Digital Signature

Based on FALCON. A lattice-based signature with very compact signature sizes. Particularly suited for constrained environments like IoT devices and embedded systems.

What About Symmetric Encryption (AES)?

AES-256 and SHA-3 are considered quantum-safe already, because Grover's Algorithm only offers a quadratic speedup against symmetric ciphers — effectively halving the key length. AES-256 remains secure even against quantum attacks.

How Does PQC Actually Work?

PQC algorithms are based on mathematical problems that are hard for both classical and quantum computers. Here are the main families:

  • Lattice-Based (ML-KEM, ML-DSA, FN-DSA): Security relies on the Learning With Errors (LWE) problem — finding a hidden structure in a high-dimensional noisy grid. Imagine finding a specific grain of sand on a beach, except the beach has millions of dimensions.
  • Hash-Based (SLH-DSA): Security relies entirely on the one-way property of cryptographic hash functions. If SHA-3 is secure, SLH-DSA is secure. Simple, conservative, and trusted.
  • Code-Based: Based on the hardness of decoding random linear error-correcting codes. This family has roots going back to 1978 (McEliece cryptosystem) — one of the longest-standing unbroken cryptographic proposals.
  • Multivariate Polynomial: Solving systems of multivariate polynomial equations over finite fields. Very compact signatures, though key sizes can be large.
  • Isogeny-Based: Uses mappings between elliptic curves. Very compact keys, but the most mathematically complex — and one candidate (SIKE) was broken in 2022, showing the need for rigorous evaluation.

Lattice-Based Crypto in Plain English

Think of a 2D grid (lattice) with a secret "shortest path" through it. The public key is the scrambled grid. The private key is the hidden shortest path. Encrypting is easy (anyone can put a message on the grid). Decrypting requires knowing the secret path. Quantum computers are no better than classical ones at finding this path — that's the magic.

The PQC Timeline

2016

NIST launches global PQC standardisation competition. 69 algorithms submitted from 25 countries.

2019–2022

Multiple rounds of evaluation. CRYSTALS-Kyber, Dilithium, FALCON, SPHINCS+ selected as finalists.

July 2022

SIKE — a popular isogeny-based candidate — is completely broken by a classical computer in under an hour, demonstrating the importance of rigorous evaluation.

August 2024

NIST publishes FIPS 203, 204, and 205 — the world's first official PQC standards. The migration era officially begins.

2025–2030

Major browsers, operating systems, cloud providers (Google, AWS, Cloudflare, Apple) roll out PQC in TLS, VPN, and app-layer encryption. Early adopters like xors.in lead the way in India.

2035 (target)

US federal systems mandated to complete full PQC migration. Global enterprises expected to follow.

What Should You Do?

PQC concerns everyone — from individuals to developers to enterprises. Here's a tailored action plan for each:

For Individuals

  • Stay informed about PQC developments — this will affect every app you use.
  • Keep software and browsers updated (browser vendors are quietly rolling out PQC).
  • Be cautious about storing highly sensitive data in long-term cloud services not yet PQC-ready.
  • Ask your bank, healthcare provider, and government portals about their PQC migration plans.
  • Prefer services with transparent security practices (like those using open standards).

For Developers

  • Learn the NIST PQC standards: ML-KEM, ML-DSA, SLH-DSA, FN-DSA.
  • Use libraries like liboqs (Open Quantum Safe project) to experiment with PQC today.
  • Design systems with crypto-agility — the ability to swap cryptographic algorithms without redesigning the whole system.
  • Prefer hybrid key exchange (classical + PQC) for new TLS implementations.
  • Audit your codebase for hard-coded RSA or ECC usages that will need migration.

For Enterprises

  • Conduct a Cryptographic Bill of Materials (CBOM) — inventory every place cryptography is used.
  • Identify and prioritise "harvest now, decrypt later" risk areas (financial data, health records, IP).
  • Partner with PQC-ready vendors and service providers (like xors.in for Indian infrastructure).
  • Begin piloting hybrid TLS 1.3 + ML-KEM in internal systems by 2025.
  • Plan for NIST-compliant PQC migration by 2030 — regulatory pressure is coming.

Don't Wait for "Quantum Supremacy" Headlines

Cryptographic migration takes years, not months. By the time a quantum computer that breaks RSA makes the news, it will be too late to start. The time to act is during the migration window — which is now.
